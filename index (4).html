01 / The Revolutionary Dilemma

The problem

Americans had just fought a war against what they viewed as distant, oppressive power. Now they needed a government capable of defending their independence—without recreating the authority they had rejected.

03 / Build the Confederation

Congress was a single-house legislature. States retained powers not expressly delegated to the United States. Test the system's rules.

Congress CAN...

Declare war and make peace; negotiate treaties; borrow money; requisition state contributions; establish postal services; manage certain western and interstate disputes; determine national coin standards.

Congress CANNOT reliably...

Tax individuals directly; regulate interstate or foreign commerce generally; compel states to supply requested funds; enforce national decisions through a separate executive or general national judiciary.

Voting rules

Each state gets one vote, regardless of population. Major measures require nine of thirteen states. Amendments require all thirteen state legislatures to agree.

04 / Evidence of Success

A weak national government was not the same as a government that accomplished nothing. Examine two consequential achievements.

Diplomacy and Independence

Congress conducted foreign affairs and ratified the Treaty of Paris in 1784, recognizing American independence. Negotiators had signed the treaty in 1783.

National Archives: Treaty of Paris

Western Lands

The Land Ordinance of 1785 established a survey system for western lands. The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 established a territorial government and a path to statehood; it prohibited slavery in the Northwest Territory, though enforcement and Native land rights remained major problems.

Historical decision simulation • 1780s

The financial emergency

War left Congress and the states with substantial debts. Soldiers, lenders, and foreign creditors expected repayment. Congress could request contributions from states, but states often sent less than requested. The national government lacked an independent taxing power. 06 / Crisis: Shays' Rebellion

Massachusetts, 1786–1787

Farmers and other protesters, including Revolutionary War veterans, challenged taxes, debt collection, and court proceedings. Some armed protesters closed courts to prevent foreclosures and imprisonment for debt. Daniel Shays became one of several leaders.

In January 1787, insurgents attempted to seize the federal arsenal at Springfield. Massachusetts forces defeated the effort; a state-funded militia ultimately suppressed the uprising.
