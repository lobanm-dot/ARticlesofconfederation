Confidential congressional briefing • Philadelphia, 1781

Can a revolution build a government that lasts?

You helped win independence from Great Britain. But rejecting a king did not settle a harder question: How much power should any national government have? Your mission is to investigate the rules BEFORE deciding what went wrong.

“Each state retains its sovereignty, freedom and independence, and every Power, Jurisdiction and right, which is not by this confederation expressly delegated to the United States, in Congress assembled.”Articles of Confederation, Article II

The case file

First, uncover why state constitutions limited government. Next, map the Confederation's structure, sort its powers, and evaluate achievements. Only then will the economic and law-and-order crises unlock in the narrative.

Key dates: Articles adopted by Congress 1777; fully ratified March 1, 1781; replaced under the Constitution in 1789.

Investigation 01 • The fear of power

Why did the states distrust strong government?

After the Declaration of Independence, states wrote their own constitutions. They agreed that legitimate government should rest on the people, but disagreed on how to organize power. Study the contrasting designs.

Exhibit A • Pennsylvania, 1776

A radical experiment

One-house legislature, an executive council rather than a powerful single governor, frequent elections, and a declaration of rights. Voting extended to adult free men meeting a tax requirement, not to everyone.

Read the 1776 Pennsylvania Constitution

Exhibit B • Massachusetts, 1780

Checks and balances

Two-house legislature, a single elected governor, a separate judiciary, and a declaration of rights. Its voting and officeholding requirements excluded many residents.

Read the Massachusetts Constitution

Investigation 02 • Blueprint for a nation

Build the government. Discover the missing pieces.

The Articles created a confederation—a union in which the states retained substantial sovereignty. Read the blueprint, then classify the features of government.

Congress

One branch

A unicameral Congress. Delegates appointed by states. Each state casts one vote, large or small.

Executive

No separate national president

Congress had presiding officers and committees, but no independent executive branch empowered to enforce national policy.

Judiciary

No general national court system

Congress could provide for certain courts and dispute processes, but lacked a permanent federal judiciary with broad jurisdiction.

Investigation 04 • The case for the defense

Was the Confederation actually a failure?

A verdict without accomplishments is incomplete. Examine concrete achievements and their limits. The Confederation Congress managed major national business—even if the system struggled elsewhere.

Evidence A • Independence

Treaty of Paris

American diplomats negotiated the Treaty of Paris in 1783. Congress ratified it in 1784, securing British recognition of independence and agreed borders—though border and British-post disputes persisted.

National Archives treaty

Evidence B • Land policy

Land Ordinance of 1785

Established a systematic method for surveying and selling western land using townships and sections; Indigenous land claims were frequently disregarded.

Library of Congress collection

Evidence C • Governing territory

Northwest Ordinance of 1787

Provided a system of territorial government and a path to equal statehood. It banned slavery in the Northwest Territory but did not settle conflicts over Native sovereignty.

Investigation 06 • Economic crisis, 1780s

Who will pay for independence?

Urgent memo to Congress

The Revolutionary War is over. Creditors, soldiers, and foreign lenders still expect payment. Congress owes money, but cannot directly tax Americans and must rely heavily on states for contributions. Many states have their own debts. Your job: keep the republic solvent without claiming nonexistent powers.

Evidence ledger

“All charges of war, and all other expences that shall be incurred for the common defence or general welfare ... shall be defrayed out of a common treasury, which shall be supplied by the several States...”Articles of Confederation, Article VIII (spelling from period text)

Congress requested money from the states. They often provided much less than requested. Congress also proposed an import duty in 1781 and a revised plan in 1783; these failed to become effective because the required state approvals were not secured.

Investigation 07 • Law, order, and legitimacy

When the courts close, who keeps the peace?

Massachusetts • 1786–1787

The farmers' grievance

Massachusetts imposed significant state taxes while many rural households struggled with scarce cash, debt, and property seizures. Protesters—including Revolutionary War veterans—demanded relief and disrupted court proceedings to prevent debt judgments and foreclosures.

Daniel Shays became one of several leaders. In January 1787, insurgents tried to seize arms at Springfield. State forces repelled them; Massachusetts raised a largely privately funded force to suppress the rebellion.

Important distinction

This rebellion occurred inside Massachusetts over state taxes and debt policies. The Articles did not directly cause every farmer's hardship. But the uprising raised wider questions about public order, debt, state responsiveness, and national capacity.

Activity 7 • Read conflicting evidence

Select highlighted evidence for each investigator perspective. The aim is to identify claims, not assume every side was completely right.

Petitioners' perspective

Many western Massachusetts protesters argued that heavy taxation, lawsuits, and debt collection threatened their farms and their independence. Some petitions asked elected officials for relief.

Liberty means more than independence from Britain: citizens need fair treatment by their own state government. But closing courts by force threatens others' rights.

Government's perspective

Massachusetts officials believed court closures and an armed attempt to seize weapons threatened republican government and the rule of law.

Elected laws and functioning courts protect order. But enforcing law without answering widespread grievances can deepen resentment.
